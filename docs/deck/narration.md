## Slide 3 — Three pathogens, three different cell envelopes

> We had planned per-protein models. Then we measured the download, and it
> changed the project.
>
> Ninety-nine point six percent of the MIC rows for these organisms are measured
> against the whole organism, not a single protein. Only thirty-five
> single-protein targets carry any MIC at all, median eight compounds each.
> There is no per-protein model to train here.
>
> So the model predicts whether a compound stops a bacterium growing, and says
> nothing about mechanism. We changed the project to fit the data rather than
> the other way round. One model per pathogen, and three deliberately different
> cell envelopes: Gram-positive, Gram-negative, and the acid-fast one, the
> hardest wall to cross with the least data behind it.

## Slide 4 — Holding out chemical scaffolds is not enough

> So how do you score something like this honestly.
>
> We cluster the compounds first and split the clusters, so no compound and no
> chemical scaffold lands on both sides. The model cannot pass by having nearly
> seen the answer.
>
> But look at what that still leaves. Removing shared ring skeletons removes
> none of the resemblance that matters. The median scaffold-test compound still
> has a close neighbour in training. The median plant compound sits far further
> out — only three point four percent of them have a near-twin, against thirteen
> point five percent of the scaffold-test ones.
>
> That distance is the whole problem. It is why we hold out every plant-derived
> compound as a third, harder set.

## Slide 5 — AUROC falls on the plant holdout for every pathogen

> Here is the model read three ways. On validation, between eighty-five and
> eighty-eight. On the scaffold-held-out test, between eighty-two and ninety.
> And on the plant holdout — what a plant screen actually asks of it — zero
> point seven-two, zero point six-eight, zero point six-three.
>
> One thing I want to be careful about. These are not a tidy descending
> staircase: *E. coli* scores higher on the scaffold test than on validation.
> The claim we can defend is narrower and still the one that matters. The plant
> holdout is consistently the hardest of the three, for every pathogen, and it
> is the only one that measures the task in the title of this project.
>
> Precision at the top of the list is what a chemist spends money against. On
> *S. aureus*, fifty-seven percent of the top tenth are active, against a
> twenty-five percent base rate. On *E. coli*, five times the hit rate of
> picking at random. Not a cure — a shorter queue for the bench.

## Slide 7 — The chemistry tracks the cell envelope

> This one we did not expect. The biosynthetic pathways of the shortlists track
> each pathogen's cell envelope. Alkaloids and terpenoids dominate the acid-fast
> list — thirty of the fifty. Peptides, sugars and fatty acids come up for the
> Gram-negative one. Shikimates and phenylpropanoids for the Gram-positive one.
>
> The model saw fingerprints and descriptors. Nobody told it about cell walls.
>
> We report it as a hypothesis worth testing, not a finding. It partly reflects
> which compounds LOTUS has annotated, and three lists of fifty cannot establish
> it. It is on this slide because the way to settle it is to go and look.

## Slide 8 — The thing we set out to show did not replicate

> We also owe you a negative result.
>
> We set out to show that holding out whole plant families is harder than
> holding out chemical scaffolds, because families share biosynthesis. We
> measured that gap four separate times.
>
> The direction is usually negative, and the magnitude never settles. On
> *S. aureus* the family split was the worse of the two in eight of ten repeats.
> On *M. tuberculosis*, seven of ten. On *E. coli* it came out the other way
> entirely. Two of these estimates rest on test sets of about a hundred
> compounds, which is probably why they say so little.
>
> So it goes in as an honest negative, not as our contribution.

## Slide 9 — What is wrong with it

> And this is the part we would rather a judge heard from us than found alone.
>
> The top-ranked candidate on all three lists is a near-copy of something in the
> training set. In two of the three it is the same non-plant polyketide, with no
> source plant and no DOI. One row ships at a Tanimoto of exactly one. Twelve,
> eight and three of the fifty rows carry no plant provenance at all. The
> per-compound attributions came out numerically impossible, so we withhold that
> column rather than show it.
>
> On the method: the labels carry publication bias, because inactive compounds
> get reported less often. A whole-organism readout gives no mechanism. Our
> curation and split stages have been independently reproduced; the model and
> shortlist stages have not. And none of these compounds has been put on a plate
> by us.
>
> A screen that only reports its wins is not a result.

## Slide 10 — Reproducibility, credit, and what is next

> Everything you have seen is read from a committed result file. Twelve pipeline
> stages, each writing its configuration hash and its metrics. The curation and
> split stages were re-run end to end from the raw downloads on a second
> machine, and all fifty-seven ledger metrics matched to the last decimal.
>
> Labels from ChEMBL, the plant edge from LOTUS, cross-checked against GBIF, all
> credited with their licences. We evaluated NPASS and dropped it: its MIC table
> is a copy of ChEMBL, so we cite ChEMBL directly.
>
> The protein layer is the obvious next build. But the honest next step is not a
> fourth pathogen — it is putting ten of these on a real plate and publishing
> the hit rate next to the predicted one, including if it is worse.
>
> And the plant records carry traditional knowledge, so anything built on this
> carries the Nagoya Protocol with it as a condition, not a footnote.
>
> Thank you.
