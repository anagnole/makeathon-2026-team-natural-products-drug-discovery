# Narration for `deck.pdf`

Read aloud over the ten slides, one block per slide.

**Total: 1,086 words, 7:29 at a measured 145 words per minute.** The cap is ten minutes.

## Who reads what

Split along what each of us actually built, so neither of us narrates the
other's work. **Leonidas takes slides 2, 3 and 7**: where the measurements come
from, what the download turned out to be, and what the chemistry of the
shortlists says. **Eleni takes 1, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9 and 10**: the model, the
evaluation, and the limits.

If Leonidas cannot record, Eleni reads all ten and nothing else changes. The
build script binds a take to a slide by its number, not by who spoke it, so the
split is a convenience, never a dependency.

Record to `video/audio/slide-NN.m4a`, one take per slide. Fluff a line and you
record that slide again. That is the entire editing process.

## Pace

Measured on the first two takes: **195 words a minute while actually speaking.**
Presentation pace is 130 to 150 and a TED talk averages about 165, so this is
fast, and it is fastest exactly where it costs most: a judge cannot hold
`0.719` and `0.682` and `0.629` if they arrive in two seconds.

**Aim for about 150.** That is not "read slowly", it is "leave the gaps in".
The words can keep their speed; the space between them is what carries.

Three registers, and the script is built out of them:

**Slow and flat** for every number, every species name, and the whole of slide
9. Land on each figure and let it sit. These are the lines that must be heard
exactly, and hurrying them is what makes a careful result sound glib.

**Steady** for the argument: most of slides 2, 3, 4, 6, 10. Even, unhurried,
no push.

**Lift** on the payoff lines, which are the only places that want energy. One
beat before each, then let it land:

- Slide 1: *That last part is the one we care about most.*
- Slide 2: *You can't look up an experiment nobody has run.*
- Slide 3: *There was nothing there to train on.*
- Slide 5: *That's not a cure. It's a shorter queue for the bench.*
- Slide 7: *Nobody ever told these models about cell walls.*
- Slide 8: *So it goes in as an honest negative. Not as our contribution.*
- Slide 9: *A screen that only reports its wins isn't a result.*
- Slide 10: *...including if it's worse.*

Eight lifts in seven minutes. If everything is emphatic, nothing is.

## The words that are hard to say

One is left, on slide 2: **Staphylococcus aureus**, staf-uh-lo-**KOK**-us
**OR**-ee-us. Everywhere else it is "*Staph*".

Every chemistry name has gone out of the spoken script. The slides print them
and the chart on slide 7 labels every class, so your voice describes the shape
and the screen supplies the vocabulary. That is the right division anyway: a
presenter who reads the labels off their own chart is giving the audience the
one thing they can already see.

## Two rules while recording

**Say the numbers as the slide prints them.** Where this script uses a short
spoken form, the printed figure follows in brackets. Say either, but do not
invent a third. Run `./video/check.py` afterwards and it reads your numbers
back against the slides.

**Nothing here is a clinical claim.** Do not add one in the moment.

## Slide 3: Three pathogens, three different cell envelopes

**Leonidas**

> In the beginning we planned to build models for specific bacterial proteins.
> Then we measured exactly what we'd downloaded, and the plan changed.
>
> We had pulled measurements for nine bacteria. Ninety-nine point six percent of
> them are against a whole living organism, not a single protein. Only
> thirty-five proteins carry any measurement at all, so there was nothing there

>
> So we changed the project to fit the data, rather than the other way around.

> nothing about mechanism.
>

> get. Gram-positive, Gram-negative, and the acid-fast one. That last one is the
> hardest wall to cross, and it has the least data behind it.

## Slide 4: Holding out chemical scaffolds is not enough

**Eleni**

> So how do you score something like this honestly?
>
> We cluster the compounds first, then we split the clusters. That way no
> compound, and no chemical skeleton, can land on both sides. The model can't
> pass by having nearly seen the answer.
>
> But that still isn't enough. Two compounds can have completely different ring
> skeletons and still look almost identical to the model.
>
> So the typical test compound still has a close relative in training. The
> typical plant compound sits much further out.
>
> Only three percent of plant compounds have a near-twin in training. For the
> test set, it's four times as many. [3.4% and 13.5%]
>
> That distance is the whole problem. It's why we hold out every plant-derived
> compound as a third, harder set.

## Slide 8: The thing we set out to show did not replicate

**Eleni**

> We also owe you a negative result.
>
> We thought we had found an even harder test. If you hold out whole plant
> families, the model never sees a relative of the thing you are asking it
> about. We expected that to be harder than holding out chemical scaffolds.
>
> We measured it four times. The effect is usually there, but it's never the
> same size, and on one of the three it disappears completely.
>
> So we're not claiming it. Our evaluation uses the split that does hold up, and
> this one goes in as an honest negative.

## Slide 9: What is wrong with it

**Eleni**

> And this is the part we want you to hear from us, not find on your own.
>
> Our own distance column caught the first one. The top candidate on all three
> lists is a near-copy of something the model already knew. That's the exact
> problem this project set out to measure, and it turned up in our own result.
>
> Then two smaller ones. Twelve, eight and three of our fifty rows have no
> source plant. And when we tried to explain the individual scores, the numbers
> came out impossible, so we held that column back.
>
> The method has limits too. Our labels are biased, because nobody publishes
> what didn't work. And none of these compounds has been near a plate yet.
>
> A screen that only reports its wins isn't a result.
